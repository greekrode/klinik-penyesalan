Rotation to Compute: CPU Leadership, Intel’s Fab Edge, and the Bond Market Warning

The AI trade is broadening beyond memory and GPUs. CPUs are back in the spotlight, Intel’s manufacturing footprint may matter more than the market thinks, and higher Treasury yields are raising the bar for every premium valuation.

27 September 20269 min readUS tech / macro

The first half of this year belonged to memory. Micron, SanDisk, SK Hynix and Samsung were the names everybody talked about: Micron’s market capitalisation nearly tripled, while the stock rallied hard as the Hormuz escalation began. In the second quarter, Micron gained more than 240%, Intel 216%, and AMD almost tripled.

Entering the third quarter, the market changed its mind. The last three months have belonged to CPU stocks. Memory has not stopped being a good business; it has become a crowded, highly anticipated one. The question is where the next leg of the AI build-out creates the most operating leverage.

The short version

AI is becoming a complete system.

GPUs generate the model’s tokens. CPUs execute the agent’s next action. The more useful AI becomes, the more compute has to happen between those tokens.

Memory might start lagging

Memory makers are still reportedly sold out of DRAM and HBM until 2027. Pricing power is the clearest sign of supply tightness: Micron’s gross margin in its last quarter was around 85%, a level we usually associate with software companies rather than a factory business.

The problem is not whether memory is good. The problem is that everybody already knows it is good.

The market has started to worry about the peak. SK Hynix and Samsung still raised DRAM prices by around 30% and more than 40%, respectively, but positioning in the memory trade is now at a stage where risk may be approaching the level of reward. When Kioxia’s CEO said prices had probably risen enough after NAND jumped 70% last quarter, investors read it as a signal that peak earnings could come in below expectations.

An investor might look at AMD’s forward P/E at around 40× while Micron sits near 7× even with record margins. That is how cyclical markets work: excellent conditions eventually create the conditions for a peak. I am not saying the peak is here. I am saying the market has begun to price the possibility.

Agentic AI brings the CPU back into play

The artificial-intelligence trade has been an Nvidia story built around GPUs for the last three years. Training large models and answering chatbot questions needed huge clusters of Nvidia chips, and the CPU was almost forgotten.

Agentic AI changes this. The GPU does the “thinking” by generating tokens—the chunks of text an AI model produces. An AI agent then has to act on that thinking: it calls tools, runs code, retrieves documents, queries databases and decides what to do next. Those tasks run on the CPU. If the CPU is slow, the expensive GPU simply waits.

Bank of America, as reported in public coverage, expects server CPU sales to grow from $61.4bn in 2026 to $210.6bn in 2030. AI CPU units could grow from 16 million in 2026E to 53 million in 2030E—roughly 35% a year. That would put CPU units at about 1.5 times the number of AI accelerators, compared with 0.7 times today. Average server CPU prices are also expected to rise from around $1,600 to $2,600.

This is not the CPU replacing the GPU. AMD CEO Lisa Su has been careful to say CPU demand is mostly additive because agents still need accelerators to run the underlying models. The AI system is getting bigger and more complete.

These are not only projections. Intel’s second-quarter revenue grew 25% to $16.1bn, its Data Center and AI business grew 59%, and hyperscaler demand was greater than the supply Intel could provide. Akamai also signed an $11.6bn, seven-year deal with Anthropic specifically for CPU workloads. Nobody signs a contract that large for seven years unless supply security matters.

Intel has the fab to prevail in the long term

My choice in the CPU group is Intel. Two years ago, Intel was the company everybody wanted to avoid. The market still looks at it mainly as a CPU turnaround story and misses its biggest difference: Intel owns its fabs.

Most competitors, including AMD, design chips and depend on TSMC to manufacture and package them. Intel can design, manufacture and package on American soil, and it is partly owned by the US government. That fab advantage opens three doors that a fabless company cannot open: selling EMIB-T packaging to other chipmakers, developing new memory technology, and potentially hosting SK Hynix memory production at Intel’s Ohio site.

1. Advanced packaging

The bottleneck in AI chips is not only making the chip; it is also packaging it. A modern accelerator combines chiplets and HBM stacks in one package, and TSMC’s CoWoS has dominated the industry. EMIB-T is Intel’s answer. Intel says it can scale to more than 12 times the reticle size with 16 or more HBM stacks in one package. Reports suggest it could be around 50% cheaper than CoWoS, with volume production in 2027. Intel has reportedly attracted Google and Nvidia for EMIB packaging.

2. A seat in the next memory architecture

Most people think Intel left memory after selling its NAND business to SK Hynix. But in February 2026, Intel and a SoftBank subsidiary signed an agreement to develop Z-Angle Memory (ZAM), a new stacked DRAM. Early reports say it could deliver around 5.3 TB/s per module versus roughly 2 TB/s per HBM4 stack. It is still far from a product, with commercialisation targeted around fiscal 2029. If the memory bottleneck lasts for years, a seat in the next architecture is valuable.

3. The potential SK Hynix deal

On 16 September, Reuters reported that SK Hynix was in talks to make memory chips in the US for the first time. It could lease part of Intel’s Ohio facility or form a joint venture with Intel and large cloud companies. Intel has put about $28bn into Ohio and struggled to find customers to fill it, so a partner like SK Hynix could turn a large cost into real income. It would also connect the manufacturing and packaging stories: SK Hynix is already exploring R&D with Intel to adapt its HBM for EMIB.

Together, you can imagine Intel becoming a US hub where CPUs, memory and advanced packaging meet—a very different story from “the old PC company.” There are important caveats. SK Hynix says no plans are finalised, the Korean government may review any deal involving sensitive HBM technology, and even a signed deal would not bring the Ohio fabs online until around 2030 or 2031.

The bond market is punishing US fiscal indiscipline

Everything above is about AI demand, and I still believe that story is strong. One thing makes me more careful, though, and it does not come from the chip sector. It comes from the bond market.

This week, the US 10-year Treasury yield touched its highest level since June 2007. The 30-year yield was already above 5.3% in mid-August, a 19-year high at the time. The Treasury tried to calm yields by doubling the size of long-dated bond buybacks in August, then tripling them to $6bn per operation in September. Yields fell for one day and then moved higher again.

Howard Marks of Oaktree wrote that Treasury buybacks are like putting an ice pack on a patient with a fever: the temperature may drop temporarily, but the patient will not get better until the cause is treated.

The causes are clear. PCE inflation was still high at 3.7% in July, almost double the Fed’s 2% target, so the Fed turned hawkish this month. The US is running a deficit of about 6% of GDP even though unemployment is only 4%. Interest payments on the debt are now larger than the US defence budget.

There is one more cause, and it is the most relevant to this note: AI itself. McKinsey estimates that more than $5tn will be spent on AI data centres through 2030. On top of that, the US needs to sell about $2tn of new debt every year. When everyone needs money at the same time, the price of money rises. The same AI boom that lifts chip stocks is also pushing rates higher.

Higher yields are not a default call. As Stanley Druckenmiller put it, higher yields are “not a crisis. It is an invoice.” The US is already paying for fiscal indiscipline slowly, through higher rates.

What surprises me is how resilient the equity market looks. The MOVE index, which measures fear in the bond market, has jumped while the VIX has barely moved. History also calls for caution: in every US midterm-election year since 1990, the equal-weight S&P 500 has fallen at least 7% from mid-August to mid-October, and we are in that window now.

What it means for positioning

Marks makes an important point: the problem is US fiscal management and potentially the dollar, not US companies. Selling good US stocks to hold dollar cash or bonds does not remove the risk because those assets carry the same dollar exposure. This is not a reason to panic.

Higher rates do change which companies deserve a premium. When money is expensive, companies that depend on borrowing suffer most, while companies already generating earnings are better protected. That fits the CPU story. For Intel, there is a nuance: building fabs is capital-intensive, which makes a partner such as SK Hynix sharing the Ohio site even more valuable. Memory sits in the middle—cheap on today’s earnings but discounted for the cycle—and Micron’s results on 30 September could close some of that gap.

For us in Indonesia, the picture is clear for now. The JCI has fallen about 30% this year and the rupiah has weakened to nearly Rp17,900 per dollar. When US yields rise and global markets get nervous, foreign money leaves the IDX and the rupiah weakens. Dollar assets protect us when things go wrong at home.

That is why I prefer to stay with the dollar and ride the AI momentum in US tech while staying light on the JCI until the rupiah stabilises. We get a currency hedge and exposure to companies leading the AI build-out—something the IDX cannot offer today. It is not risk-free: a rupiah recovery would reduce dollar returns, and AI stocks can fall quickly when equities turn fearful.

For now: stay with the US dollar and AI winners that earn money today, stay light on the JCI, and keep watching the bond market.
